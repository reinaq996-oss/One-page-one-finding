Grades can signal performance. They should never define potential.

A study of 649 students from two Portuguese secondary schools, and of what we should refuse to use when we judge them.

Our question

What predicts a final grade, and which of those things would you refuse to use?

Start with the strongest signal

A final grade says how a student performed. It can’t say why.

We took grades, habits and family background for 649 students and asked what lines up with the final result, and what we would never want to hold against a student.

Last term’s grades say the most.

Earlier grades from the same year track the final grade closely: 0.92 for the second period, 0.83 for the first. Background factors sit far lower, and students don’t choose them.

88.9% finished within one point of their second-period grade.

290 rose287 unchanged72 fell

That is continuity over a year, not a verdict on what a student can do next.

Grades rise with study time, then level off.

Students who report 5 to 10 hours a week average 13.23, against 10.84 for under two hours. The smallest group dips slightly, but it holds only 35 students. This is an association, not proof that study time causes better grades.

Background shows up in the averages. It isn’t the student.

Grades rise with parents’ education, from 10.80 with primary-educated mothers to 13.07 with higher-educated ones. A student can’t choose that, so it describes where they start, not their effort or ability.

Even the address gap is partly something else. Urban students average 1.17 points higher overall, but only about 0.54 within each school. Part of the “address effect” is really a school effect.

Strong signal is not the same as fair to use.

Two questions, asked separately: how strongly is a factor linked to the grade, and is it fair to judge a student by it? The answer to the second depends on the decision being made.

What this analysis can’t tell you.

Group averages do not describe every individual, and a few of the patterns rest on very few students.

  • Association is not causation.

    Students who receive extra school support score lower on average, but they may already be the ones struggling. The data cannot tell the two stories apart.

  • Some groups are tiny.

    Only six students have mothers with no formal education. Their average is too unstable to read.

  • 15 zero grades, no explanation.

    All 15 have zero recorded absences, 14 attend school MS, and 7 also scored zero in G2. We kept them in, and checked that the main pattern holds without them.

  • Two schools, one subject.

    These are Portuguese-language grades from two secondary schools in Portugal. The patterns may not hold elsewhere.
